# Human settlements: what history teaches, and ideas for Drawn Borders

Research for Maz (October 2026). Part 1 is what I learned, in short. Part 2 is the ideas, each tied to something the game already has. Part 3 is my recommendation for where to start.

---

## Part 1: What I learned

### 1. How settlements began
- For most of human history people had **no fixed home**: small groups followed animals and wild food.
- Around **10,000 BC** farming and herding let people **stay in one place**. The first villages in the Middle East were put **where several kinds of land meet** (hills, valley, water), so people could use all of them. One of the first buildings was a **granary**: a village exists because it can **store food**.
- Big towns came early: **Çatalhöyük** (Turkey) had thousands of people around 7500 BC.
- The first **cities** appeared around **3500 BC** in southern Iraq (Sumer). By 3000 BC there were cities from China to Peru.
- At Göbekli Tepe, big **temples came before towns**: belief and meeting places can pull people together before farming does.

**Lesson:** a settlement starts where there is **food, water, safety, and a reason to meet**.

### 2. Why a settlement is born in one place (site and situation)
Geographers split this in two:
- **Site** = the ground itself. **Situation** = what is around it (other towns, roads, rivers, borders).

The classic reasons for a site:
| Kind of site | What it means | Real example |
|---|---|---|
| Wet point | Reliable water in dry land (spring, well, river) | Oasis towns, Kingston upon Thames |
| Dry point | Dry high ground inside marsh or floodplain | Ely |
| Defensive | Hill, or inside a river bend | Durham, Shrewsbury |
| Bridging point / ford | Where a river can be crossed; the **lowest crossing** before the sea often becomes a great city | London, Oxford |
| Gap town | A pass through hills or mountains | Lincoln |
| River mouth / harbor | Sea meets river: trade both ways | Most great ports |
| Crossroads | Roads meet: a market grows | Many market towns |
| Resource | Gold, coal, oil, fish, timber | Gold-rush towns |
| Sacred | A temple or holy place | Mecca, Varanasi |
| Shelter / aspect | Sunny, protected slope | Mountain villages |

### 3. Shapes of settlements
- **Dispersed**: lonely farms far apart (needs lots of land, harsh climate, or safe times).
- **Nucleated** (clustered): houses packed around a church, a well, a market or a crossroads (for defence, water, sharing work).
- **Linear**: a line along a road, river or valley.
- In unsafe times people cluster behind walls; in safe times they spread out.

### 4. How settlements grow
- **Food reach limits size.** A city can only be as big as the food it can bring in. Before railways, carrying grain by land was very expensive, so big cities were almost always on **a sea or a big river**. Ancient Rome fed about a million people with grain shipped from Egypt and North Africa.
- **Von Thünen's rings**: around a market city, farming arranges itself by transport cost: fresh food and milk closest, then firewood and timber, then grain, then cattle farthest away. A river or a railway stretches the rings along it.
- **Travel time limits a city's area (Marchetti's constant).** People everywhere spend about **one hour a day** travelling. Walking at 5 km/h means a city about 2.5 km across its radius. Rome never grew past about 4 km wide. Trams pushed cities to about 6 km, cars to 20 km and more. **Faster transport makes cities spread, not commutes shorter.**
- **Inside the city**: rings (centre, factories, workers' homes, rich suburbs — Burgess), wedges along railways and rivers (Hoyt), and with cars, many centres (Harris and Ullman): suburbs and "edge cities".
- **Bigger cities are more productive per person.** Researchers (Bettencourt, West) found that when a city doubles, wages, wealth and new inventions per person go up about **15%**, while roads and pipes per person go **down** about 15% (bigger is cheaper to serve). But crowding problems (disease, crime) also rise.
- **The urban graveyard.** Before about 1800, cities had so much disease (dirty water, crowding) that **more people died than were born**. Cities only grew because villagers kept moving in. Clean water and sewers ended this, and by the 1930s the "city death penalty" was gone.

### 5. The hierarchy of places
- **Central place theory (Christaller)**: many small villages, fewer towns, a few cities, one capital. Each level offers services to the level below: a village has a shop, a town has a market and a doctor, a city has a hospital and a university, the capital has everything. Places of the same level are spaced evenly.
- **Rank-size rule (Zipf)**: the 2nd city is about half the 1st, the 3rd a third, and so on.
- **Primate city**: in many countries the capital is **far bigger** than the rule says (Paris, London, Bangkok) and dominates national life. The second city often resents it.

### 6. How people move
- **Push and pull**: people are pushed out (no work, hunger, war, no land) and pulled in (jobs, wages, safety, family already there).
- **Ravenstein's laws** (from 1880s censuses): most moves are **short**; people move **in steps** (village → town → city); **every flow makes a smaller counter-flow** back; the main direction is **country to city**.
- **Chain migration**: people follow relatives and neighbours from their home village, so towns become linked to particular villages or regions.
- **Slums**: when people arrive faster than houses are built, informal quarters grow. Today about 1.1 billion people live in slums (about a quarter of city people in the world, half in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia). A slum lacks clean water, toilets, space, solid houses, or legal right to stay.

### 7. How settlements change their people
- City people have **fewer children**, more schooling, more trades and professions, and less traditional religion.
- Cities make **new ideas and new politics**: unions, merchants' power, revolutions often start in cities.
- Villages keep tradition, land, family ties.
- A town's main job shapes its people: a mining town is young and male, rough and well paid; a port town is mixed and open; a university town is young and educated.

### 8. How people change their settlements
- They build walls, temples, markets, bridges, harbors, sewers, railways — and each changes what the town can become.
- Big plans: **planned cities and capitals** (Washington, Canberra, Brasília built far from any city, Islamabad, Abuja), British "new towns" and garden cities after the wars.
- **Company towns**: a mine or factory builds a whole town for its workers.
- Disasters and rebuilding: the Great Fire of London led to brick houses and building rules.

### 9. How settlements decline and die
- **Boomtowns** grow overnight on gold, silver or oil, and many die when it runs out (ghost towns). Timber towns die when the forest is cut.
- **Shrinking cities**: Detroit lost **61% of its people** from 1950 to 2010 (car industry left, suburbs, segregation). A vicious circle: people leave → less tax → worse services → more people leave. More than **a quarter of big cities in the world are shrinking**. Shrinking spreads outward like waves; "right-sizing" (making the city smaller on purpose) helps but is not enough alone.
- Also: a river changes course, a harbor silts up, a trade route moves, a capital moves away, plague, war.

### 10. How stubborn cities are
- **Cities keep going after their reason is gone.** In the USA, towns grew where boats had to be carried around waterfalls (portage). The portage stopped mattering 150 years ago, yet those cities are still big (Bleakley and Lin). Once people, buildings and businesses are there, they hold each other in place.
- **Cities come back from destruction.** Japan's bombed cities (half of their buildings destroyed, Hiroshima losing over 20% of its people at once) were back on their old growth path within about 20 years; Hiroshima's centre had its density back in 5. German bombed cities also recovered (the effect was real but temporary).

**Lesson for a game:** geography decides where a town is born; momentum and people decide whether it lives.

## Part 2: Ideas for the game

Each idea: **what it is in real life → what it would be in Drawn Borders → what it builds on**. The game already has: towns founded as projects, villages that appear by themselves (show only), migration for work and hunger, importance, roles, mood, loyalty, age, city programs, provinces, people and skills, eras.

### A. Places that are born by themselves (the WorldBox feeling)
1. **Villages that grow into towns** (already planned as provinces Part 3). Make it follow real rules: a village grows faster on a good **site** (water, a ford, a crossroads, a harbor, a road passing by) and slower on a bad one. Steps: hamlet → village → market town. When a village is big enough and has a good site, a **"A town wants a charter"** card: grant it (it becomes a real town with a name, governor's office, its own role) or refuse (it stays a village under the nearest town).
   *Builds on:* `updateVillages`, villages' kind (farm, mine, fish), roads, the town-founding project.
2. **Towns where roads meet.** Every road the player builds can make a crossroads; after some years a market village appears there by itself. Railways later make **railway junction towns** — and towns the railway passes by start to shrink.
   *Builds on:* roads, importance (crossroads already counts).
3. **Frontier settlers.** A "settlers" or "homestead" law: free land for families who go to the wild land of your country. Villages spread into empty provinces (territories) faster, the farmers' group likes it, but the new villages are poor and far from the capital at first.
   *Builds on:* territories, villages, farmers' group.

### B. Where you put a town really matters
4. **A site report when founding a town.** Before you found a town, show its site like a geography teacher: "Dry point on a river, lowest crossing before the sea, good farmland, open to attack from the east." Each line changes **how big it can grow, how fast, and how hard it is to take**. The player starts to think like a real founder.
   *Builds on:* the founding preview, importance lines (river, harbor, high ground are already there).
5. **Food reach limits city size.** Every town gets a **food shed**: how far food can come to it, by road, river, sea, and later rail. A town can only grow as big as the farmland in its food shed can feed. Inland towns with no river stay small until railways — exactly like history. A map mode draws the food shed and the von Thünen rings (gardens and milk close, grain further, herds far out).
   *Builds on:* the food ratio per network, roads, the sea and rivers, trade (imported grain by sea feeds a port city — Rome's grain fleet).
6. **Travel time limits city area.** The city's drawn size (and its room for houses) depends on the era's transport: walking city → tram city → car city. A city that has more people than its area can hold gets **crowding**: housing crisis, disease, slums. Building trams or an underground (already a decision: "the mayor's grand plan") **really** opens room to grow. Later, **suburbs spill into nearby cells** (already on the ideas list).
   *Builds on:* city drawing (v25–v29), eras, the housing-shortage mood cause.

### C. Growth and its price
7. **The urban graveyard.** In early eras big cities have more deaths than births: they grow **only by newcomers from villages**. A **"clean water and sewers"** city project ends it (and is a great work in a big city). Without it, cities above a size get **epidemics** that spread along roads and sea lanes. This makes the countryside matter: villages feed cities with people as well as food.
   *Builds on:* births, deaths and life expectancy (v33), healthcare, migration, great works.
8. **Slums (informal quarters).** When newcomers arrive faster than the city builds homes, a slum grows on the map at the city's edge. It gives cheap workers (good for factories) but lowers mood, raises disease and fire, and angers the workers' group. Choices: clear it (people leave, anger), upgrade it (water, land titles: costs money, slowly becomes a normal quarter), or ignore it. The People panel shows "people in slums" and the world rank.
   *Builds on:* the housing crisis and newcomers' quarter decisions, mood, people panel.
9. **Real big-city effects.** Make the big-city bonus follow the real rule: each doubling = more wages, research and new businesses per person, cheaper roads per person — **and** more crime and disease, less belonging. Show it on the city file: "Big city: +11% wages, +9% inventions, crime +12%".
   *Builds on:* the big-city bonus (now +2.5% for each doubling, at most +6%), research, mood.

### D. A real ladder of places
10. **Services by level (central places).** Each level of town gives services to the land around it: villages a shop, market towns a market and a doctor, cities a hospital, regional centres a university and courts, the capital everything. A village far from any market town is poorer and less happy; a province with no city has no hospital. This gives **a reason to found towns in the right places**, spaced out, and makes provinces matter more.
    *Builds on:* provinces, the needs report, governors, importance classes.
11. **The capital that eats the country (primate city).** Statistics shows your nation's **city ladder** (rank against size, with the rank-size line). If the capital grows far above the line, it gets richer and draws the educated, but the second city and the provinces resent it (loyalty, the "second city" decision becomes more likely), and the countryside empties. Policies can push either way: spend in the provinces (governors' money) or build up the capital.
    *Builds on:* governors' money, the "second city that wants what the capital has" decision, Statistics.
12. **Two towns that grow together.** When two cities grow until their land touches, they become one metropolitan area (like the Ruhr or Minneapolis–St Paul): bigger city effects, but one city's pride, the other's resentment, and a card about joining them.

### E. People and places shape each other
13. **Urbanisation drives politics.** A new main number: **share of people living in towns**. As it rises: fewer children, more schooling, the workers' and business groups grow, faith and farmers shrink. Most of these already happen; making urbanisation the visible cause would tell the player the story of their nation's modernisation.
    *Builds on:* groups' power (village people already feed faith), children per woman.
14. **Migration that follows real laws.** Most moves short; people move in steps (village → town → city); each flow makes a smaller counter-flow home; **chain migration** — a town keeps receiving people from the same villages and regions, shown as "where its people come from". In the migration arrows on the map you could see the old home links.
    *Builds on:* `migrateNation` (moves for work and hunger now), the map's moving people.
15. **Town character from its job.** A mining town is young, mostly men, rough, well paid, with strikes; a port town mixed and open to new ideas; a university town young and noisy (students' march already exists); a farm town old and traditional. These would change the town's group mix, mood causes and events.
    *Builds on:* roles, groups, people's ages.

### F. Special kinds of settlements
16. **Boomtowns and ghost towns.** A newly opened gold or oil field pulls people in very fast (high wages, crowding, crime). When the mine runs out: diversify (a program, costly), or the town shrinks; if it falls very low it becomes a **ghost town** — a ruin left on the map with its name and history. (The "mine town's last shift" decision is already there; this makes it the end of a full story.)
    *Builds on:* deposits that run down, mining town role, city history.
17. **Shrinking cities.** The vicious circle: people leave → less tax → worse services → more leave. A shrinking city shows the spiral in its file; the player can "right-size" (pull services in, demolish empty quarters, lower costs) or fight it with an industrial program.
    *Builds on:* the shrinking-town mood cause, city programs.
18. **Company towns.** A big mine, steelworks or oil field can come with a company town: built fast and cheap by business, loyal to the company, angry at the first strike.
19. **A brand-new planned capital** (Brasília, Canberra, Washington): instead of moving the capital to an old city, build a new one in the middle of the country. Very expensive and slow, but it binds the provinces together (loyalty up in far provinces, the old capital loses pride).
    *Builds on:* moving the capital, the town project.
20. **Holy cities and pilgrims.** A temple or shrine great work can turn a town into a pilgrimage centre: visitors each year, the faith group's pride, a target in war that shocks the world.

### G. Stubborn cities
21. **Momentum.** A city keeps much of its importance after its reason is gone (the mine closed, the harbor silted up): its people, buildings and businesses hold it. Model it as "city momentum" that fades slowly, so a city never collapses the season its mine closes.
22. **Rebuilding after war.** Cities hit by war (street battles, sieges, bombing in later eras) lose people and buildings but bounce back fast if the site is good and the people come home: a "rebuild" card (fast and cheap, or slow with better planning — wider streets, brick instead of wood, like London after 1666).
    *Builds on:* battles fought in a city (in its history), loyalty for freed cities.
23. **Disasters by site.** Wet-point and floodplain towns flood, old wooden towns burn (fire in the old town exists), coasts get storms. Defences (dykes, fire brigades) are city projects. The site you chose at founding comes back years later.

## Part 3: My recommendation

Following how we work (big ideas in parts you can play between):

- **Part 1 — "Places that live" (best first):** ideas **1, 2, 4, 16** — villages that grow into towns by site rules and ask for a charter, market villages at crossroads, the site report when founding a town, boomtowns and ghost towns. This finishes the planned provinces Part 3, gives the WorldBox and OpenTTD feeling (connect places and watch them grow), and touches the existing code the least.
- **Part 2 — "Limits to growth":** ideas **5, 6, 7, 8** — food shed, city area by transport era, the urban graveyard and sewers, slums. This is the most realistic part and makes geography and eras truly decide city size.
- **Part 3 — "A ladder of places":** ideas **10, 11, 13, 14** — services by level, the capital that eats the country, urbanisation as a main number, migration by real laws.
- Later: the special towns (17–20) and stubborn cities (21–23), as small additions.
