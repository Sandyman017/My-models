# Drawn Borders — game plan

## What the player wants (Maz)
- Loves: HOI (army management), WorldBox (see the world change as nations act), OpenTTD (connect cities and watch them grow).
- Economy, relations and army are the most fun parts. Seasons should be slow and thoughtful; decisions must matter.
- Loves statistics: wants to know everything going on, for their nation, other nations and the world.
- Wanted a UN-like assembly: a nation opens a topic with a statement, and every country replies under it like a group chat.
- Wants the map to look realistic (said the old map looked ugly). OK to use external resources; ask permission first if something better needs them.
- Found the interface crowded (sidebar took half the screen); wants a clean screen with small round buttons that pop up panels.
- War: both watch it play out on the map and direct armies yourself.
- Disliked: dashboard look and sliders. Wants a game look with the map at the center.
- Wants rivals to act realistically, in their own national interest, like real politics.
- Plays on PC with a mouse. English UI (English is a second language: keep explanations simple). Wants Claude to go beyond the literal ask and do what's best for the game.
- Wants every economic sector to really affect the game, not just exist.
- Said the game "is still boring" and not yet the dream; will raise issues one by one. First: the economy should come from geography (click a gold deposit, see how much is there, build a road to it, build a mine, see the workers, watch the gold run down). Wants realism and logic, "not adding for the sake of adding", and is happy for this to take a long time.
- Then asked to look up what makes the most popular strategy games loved and take small aspects from each, "in your genius way" — not everything.
- Asked to see the whole code and its size (sent the file; 13,941 lines at v16), and whether there is a limit (none close: published pages can be 16 MB).
- Asked whether outside graphics could make the game look much better; was offered (1) photo textures for the map, (2) professional icons, (3) pictures for decisions, eras and leaders, (4) a faster WebGL renderer. Chose 1 + 2 (done in v17). Wants to be told the plan before big changes like this.
- Lost worlds when the game was updated: wants saving to be safe (done in v18).
- The sea: "I get goosebumps from the idea of having soldiers/people moving to far cities by the sea when needed." Wants it done perfectly: water more valuable, correct numbers with a purpose (not random people moving from town A to town B), and no country gets it for free (build ports, build or buy ships), and it must make sense. Chose: saves first, then the sea; fleets commanded by hand or handed to admirals; all of it (ports, freighters, warships, sea zones, blockades, sea battles, rivers).
- Asked for the game's size again at v19 (17,458 lines, about 1.4 MB) and was surprised it is so small (code is text; the game draws everything itself).
- Then asked for "more depth in small changes over everything" to make the game more interesting and playable, with recommendations before starting. Eleven ideas were offered and Maz said "Go": seven in v20; the other four (town character, interest groups, long memory, coalitions) wait.
- Ministries (his own idea): establish ministries (interior, commerce, agriculture, defence, energy were his examples), each with a minister he can talk to; through the discussion he gives orders that really affect the ministry, the country or the world; a ministries icon with a list, and a page per ministry with the minister's info. Chose ministries only for v21 ("we will do interest groups later"), and said not to spend effort on old saves this time: he starts a new world to get the full experience.
- After v21 he said the game "is still so boring": he opened it for five minutes and could not keep playing, and did not know exactly why. Found by playing: nothing happened to the player for years (rivals far away, no war allowed for two years), a wait of about 5 seconds and a 220–320 word report every season, and no goal. Of four fixes he chose the first three (done in v22): time that runs with pause, short news instead of the long report, and a goal and a danger from the first minute. The fourth, guided tips, was not chosen.

- After v22 he found the ministers useless: the Economy minister could not say if we import electronics, Defence knew nothing of other armies, Foreign Affairs sent him to Defence. Wants ministers like real ones who give information and intelligence and know their job. Said to do everything needed, without limits (v23).
- Has loved exploring Google Maps for years (terrain, cities, forests, deserts) and wants the game world to look like a satellite view and be beautiful (not the real Earth: the game's own world). Asked why the game is limited to 16 MB (it is the artifact page limit, also for private pages; extra files can add more).
- Then asked for a big upgrade of cities (v31): each city with its own importance from real-life factors (geography, population, minerals and more, "you know better than me"), so that managing cities is not repetitive or identical and cities weigh more. Said not to ask for his opinion or permission that time: he wanted to come back hours later to a finished version.
- After v31 he asked for recommendations and chose interest groups (v32), with crises but no elections yet, finished without questions. Confirmed the map works fine on his PC.
- After v32 he asked for more depth in people's jobs and everything connected; after my recommendations he chose the full package (v33): kinds of workers, wages and living standards, generations, labour laws. Immigration and machines replacing workers were offered for later.
- After v38 he asked me to research the history of human settlements on the internet (how they are born, grow, shape their people and are shaped by them, their kinds) and come back with ideas; the research is in `settlements-research.md`. He chose "Places that live" and said this part matters very much to him: every village with its own people and look (he sent five satellite pictures: a village among trees with red roofs, a street village along a winding stream, walled yards with round huts in dry land, a packed village by a great river, a modern village with curving closes), the rest of that package, and at least 3000 lines of real work, "honest and the best work". Built as v39.
## Decisions
- One turn = one season. Since v22 time runs by itself (a season every 36, 18 or 8 seconds), pauses with Space and pauses by itself when something needs the player; the engine still works season by season. Single self-contained HTML, plain JS, canvas map. Published artifact declares the `sample` capability (Claude), and since v18 also `db` + `user` (saves in the player's account) and `downloads` (save files).
- Code owns all rules and numbers; Claude only chooses among legal options and writes words. Output validated; built-in rules always available as fallback.
- Player text (letters, Assembly speeches) is untrusted in-world text: it never changes decisions; Claude writes replies but cannot change the outcome.
- Rival personalities randomized from archetypes: Expansionist, Mercantile, Agrarian, Militarist. Open-ended game (legacy ranking, no forced victory).
- Interface is map-first: the map fills the screen; everything else floats over it and opens on demand.
- Natural resources are places on the map, not town buildings: mines, oil fields, logging camps and fishing harbors are opened at a deposit, forest or coast.
- Every choice shows its exact consequences before the player commits (decisions, works, ambitions, war risk).
- Outside art only with clear free licenses (CC0, or CC BY with an in-game credit), embedded inside the one file so the downloaded game still works offline. (The workspace can't reach most art sites; GitHub works.) Since v24 the map is painted entirely by the game (the v17 ground photographs were removed).
- Saves must always load in later versions: the world is rebuilt from its seed, so world generation must not change, and new systems must fill in sensible defaults for older saves.
- The sea is derived from the world (zones, rivers, coasts), never stored; new harbors only on the open sea; everything moved by sea is counted in tons of shipping.
- Depth comes from small systems that work on each other (jobs, people moving, food, war), each explained in the game, not from more buttons. Since v20 every number in the top bar explains itself when hovered.

### Version 16: ideas from the best strategy games (Maz: "take small aspects from each game that make it special")
Researched what players love in the best-known strategy games and borrowed one idea from each, adapted to this game.
- **Ambitions** (Hearts of Iron focus trees, Europa Universalis missions, Old World ambitions). Three offers drawn from your real situation (join every town by road, become self-sufficient in a good, open more mines, grow the economy, pay off debt, sign a trade deal, win a friend, pass an Assembly motion, field more divisions, outgrow a neighbor's army, free or take a town in war, complete a great work, research a technology). Two active at a time, each with a deadline. Fulfilled: prestige plus a concrete reward. Missed: −3 stability for 4 seasons and −3 prestige.
- **Decisions** (Crusader Kings and Stellaris events, Frostpunk dilemmas, Old World conditional events). 20 situations that only happen when their conditions are real: miners' strike, mine collapse (option: a mine safety law), gold rush, oil spill near fishing grounds, wildfire, drought, general strike, refugees from a nearby war, border incident (can give a just cause), foreign investors, epidemic, a brilliant scientist, corruption scandal (a cover-up may come out later), record harvest, returning veterans, empty nets, bare hills, river flood (levees), commodity windfall, and the succession. Each option shows its exact effects as chips. Undecided after 2 seasons: the ministers pick the default. Keys 1–3 choose; Esc puts it off.
- **Storyteller** (RimWorld). The chance of a new situation grows the longer it has been quiet; a nation in trouble is spared new disasters; bad news is more often followed by good. About one decision a year. Rivals face their own and choose by character.
- **Great works** (Civilization wonders). Ten, tied to geography or development: Great Dam (big river in hills), Transcontinental Railway (5+ joined towns spanning 24+ cells), Grand Harbor (port), National Academy of Sciences, Stock Exchange, World's Fair, Line of Fortresses (border with a great power), National Monument, National Broadcasting Tower, Atomic Project. One per world; paid each season; the first to finish wins and the others get half back. Works standing on land belong to whoever holds it. Landmarks on the map. Rivals race for them (in simulations they finish 6–9 of 10 within 15 years).
- **World firsts, eras and legacy** (Civilization historic moments, Humankind fame). Prestige from world firsts (each technology, economy and population milestones, ten towns, six sites, three trade partners, twenty divisions, skilled workforce), great works, ambitions, Assembly motions and leaders' titles. World eras: Steam → Electricity → Radio → Electronic → Atomic → Information. Statistics → Legacy: ranking, prestige chart, great works, world firsts and the hard choices every government made.
- **Your leader** (Crusader Kings, Old World). The player has a leader with two traits, each with a real effect (for example cautious: armies defend 10% harder; honorable: +5 opinion with all; greedy: +5% tax, −2 stability). Leaders earn titles by deeds ("the Builder", "the Conqueror", "the Great", "the Prosperous", "the Wise", "the Founder", "the Shield", "the Peacemaker"); rivals too. When yours dies or retires, you choose among three candidates.
- **War-risk intelligence** (Into the Breach: show the enemy's intentions). Every bordering great power shows a war risk (low / watchful / high / imminent) from the same calculation its government uses, with what draws them to war, what holds them back and what would deter them. The dispatch warns when the risk rises.
- **Coming up** (Civilization's "one more turn"). A small list above End season: decisions waiting, projects, research, great works and ambitions with seasons left.
- Interface: new Agenda panel (flag icon). Panel keys are now 1 Realm, 2 Agenda, 3 Economy, 4 Army, 5 Inspect, 6 Nations, 7 Letters, 8 Log (9 Navy since v19). Prestige in the top bar; the era under the season.
- Version 15 saves load: the player gets a leader, and world firsts already reached are recorded quietly.
- Checked by an independent code review (10 bugs found and fixed) plus headless simulations and browser tests.

### Version 17: photo map and professional icons (Maz chose options 1 + 2)
- **Photo textures on the map.** Seven CC0 ground photographs (rock, meadow, dry ground, hill soil, forest floor, sand, snow) by Nobiax (yughues) and rubberduck, prepared by CryHam for the open-source game Stunt Rally (taken from its GitHub repository). Flattened so they tile, 256×256 WebP, embedded as data URIs (about 220 KB).

Rock and forest photos also add relief: crags in the mountains and tree crowns with shadows. The crags get lower as you zoom closer, so ridges and snowfields stay visible at every zoom. Snow and rock placement follow the land's own shape, not the crags.

- **Icons from game-icons.net** (CC BY 3.0: Lorc, Delapouite, Skoll, sbed, Willdabeast, Faithtoken), 48 in all, replacing the old line icons everywhere. Examples: crowned coin (treasury), three people (population), grain sheaf (food), scales (stability), crossed swords (army), laurel crown (prestige), flying flag (Agenda), quill (Letters), house (found town), pickaxe (resources view).
- On the map: site badges (mine pickaxe, coal cart, gold bars, oil pump, logging axe, fishing hook), great-work landmarks (waterfall dam, steam locomotive, lighthouse, graduate cap, bank, fair pavilion, stone tower, triumphal arch, radio tower, atom), port anchors, and known deposits shown as small ore / coal / oil-drop / gold-nugget icons instead of dots. Map icons are cached as small pictures so drawing them every frame stays cheap.
- **Credits** button (bottom left, next to Motion) opens a card crediting the icons, the photographs and the fonts.

### Version 18: saves that are never lost (Maz: "sometimes when you make a change the old save gets deleted and I start with a new world")
- The cause: each time the save format changed, the game threw away saves in the old format; and starting a new world deleted the save. Saves also lived only in one browser.
- **Saved games window** (floppy-disk button at the bottom of the left dock, or the L key; also "Load a saved game" on the founding and collapse screens).
- **Autosave**: every action is saved in the browser at once; at the end of every season the game is also saved to the player's Claude account (the artifact's private per-user storage), with the season before kept as a second copy. On another browser or computer the account save is picked up at start.
- **Named saves** (up to 10), load, delete (with a confirmation); **download a save as a file** and **open one from a file**.
- **Nothing is deleted by accident**: "New world" keeps the old game as an "earlier game" (up to 3); loading another save first keeps a "before loading" copy; a save the game cannot read is set aside, never overwritten.
- Saves are packed (gzip) and split into pieces under the storage limit; a piece is written before the index points to it, so a broken upload never spoils a save. If the account can't be reached, the browser save still works and the window says so.
- Every save carries its game id, version and format number; version 18 reads version 15–17 saves, and every later version must read older saves too.
- File 1.21 MB, 14,865 lines. Checked with 40 save tests (account, second browser, new world, loading, old saves, files, damaged saves, very large saves, storage outage) plus the full browser test suite.

### Version 19: the sea (Maz: "soldiers and people moving to far cities by sea when needed", done "perfectly", with real numbers, and nothing given for free)
- **Sea zones** worked out from the map itself (so old worlds keep their seas): the open ocean is split into about 15–20 named zones ("Peline Sea", "Gulf of Estmere", "Bay of Noris"); lakes are closed water. The strongest fleet at sea in a zone holds it; the map shows the zones, their names and who holds them while the Navy panel is open or a fleet is selected.
- **Harbors in three levels.** Harbor ($12B, 1 season): sea trade and the world market, fishing base, buying freighters abroad, room for 20 Mt of trade a year, 2 troopship berths. Port and shipyard (+$10B, 2 seasons, needs 300,000 people): 50 Mt, 5 berths, 4 slipways for freighters and destroyers. Naval base (+$16B, 3 seasons): 80 Mt, 8 berths, 8 slipways that also build cruisers, fast repairs, shore batteries (×1.3 against landings; ×1.15 for its fleet in battle). Upkeep $250M / $400M / $700M a season. New harbors only on the open sea.
- **Ships with real models.** Freighter (Liberty ship: 10,000 t cargo, 11 knots, crew 44, $40M, 1 season; $60M second-hand from shipowners abroad, at most 6 a season). Destroyer (Fletcher class: 2,050 t, 36 knots, crew 330, $600M, 3 seasons). Cruiser (Cleveland class: 11,800 t, 32 knots, crew 1,250, $1.6B, 6 seasons). Warships can also be bought from trade partners and allies with yards (30% dearer, the money goes to them, it uses room in their yards). Ships burn fuel oil; a navy short of oil fights worse.
- **No nation starts with ships**, rivals included: they build harbors, enlarge them, lay down ships or buy freighters by character, wealth and threat (mercantile and militarist powers build the biggest navies; city-states at most a few destroyers).
- **Fleets on the map**: click a fleet, right-click the sea to send it (any sea in one season), right-click your harbor to dock. Missions: patrol (holds the sea, guards convoys, blockades) or raid shipping (sinks merchant ships, slips away from bigger fleets). Join and split fleets. Or hand them to the admirals, with an order to guard your waters or seek battle; in peace they keep the ships in harbor, in war they escort convoys, guard ports, blockade the enemy's main port and send destroyers raiding; a much weaker fleet stays in harbor.
- **Sea battles explained like land battles**: strength from guns and hull, readiness, every factor listed (near your naval base, far from any port, short of fuel, radar from electronics, planes from the shore, luck); the loser's ships run for home; sunk and damaged ships named. Damaged ships repair in harbor, fastest at a naval base.
- **Blockades**: an enemy holding the waters of your harbor stops its trade, fishing and ships; counts in the war score like ground taken (+4 a port) and costs stability (−1.5 a port, up to −5).
- **Troops by sea, by the tonnage**: 5 tons of shipping per infantryman into a port (7 artillery, 12 armor), ×1.8 for a landing on a beach; so an infantry division of 15,000 needs 8 freighters to a harbor, about 14 to land on a beach. Loading at 1,500 t a day per berth, sailing at 11 knots, unloading at the far harbor's berths or over a beach (4 days for the first waves, then about 8,000 t a day). Select divisions and right-click a coast or a harbor across the sea: they march to the best harbor, board and sail; the game picks the sea when it is faster (Shift+right-click forces it) and shows freighters, days and dangers. A voyage longer than a season arrives the next. At most 3 divisions land on one beach a season; troops in training don't sail. Enemy warships on the way sink freighters and soldiers, or turn the convoy back. Landings against defenders fight at half strength, helped by cruisers' gunfire.
- **Supply by sea**: troops overseas are supplied from any harbor of yours ships can leave (1 freighter a division, 2 for armor). **Towns cut off by water** join the national market when they have a harbor and 4 freighters per million people are free.
- **Trade by sea needs ships and room**: goods are counted in tons (food 1 t, coal 4, oil 2.5, electronics almost nothing). Foreign shipowners carry what your own freighters don't, for $25 a ton; in war with a nation that has warships most of them stay away (and charge three times as much), so your own freighters keep trade alive. Full harbors hold trade back. Your freighters earn "Shipping" income.
- **Overseas towns**: found a town on a wild coast your ships can reach; settlers sail on 2 freighters.
- **Rivers**: towns on the same big river with a wharf ($3B) are joined by barges as if by road. Every river (class 2+) without a road bridge costs armies time to cross (+1 move, +1.5 for a great river) and weakens attacks across it (×0.75, ×0.65 great river, ×0.9 fighting over a bridge).
- **War across the sea**: you can declare war on any nation your ships can reach. Rivals still go to war only with neighbors, but fight at sea and land troops once at war.
- Interface: Navy panel (anchor button next to the Army, key 9): overview, selected fleet, admirals, fleet list, merchant fleet and what it is doing, buying freighters, shipyards with build buttons, buying warships abroad, harbors with "enlarge", your waters, last season's sea battles. Town panel: "Harbor and river" section. Dispatch: "At sea" with sea battle reports. Playback: fleets sail, convoys cross, battles splash before the armies march. Statistics: warship and freighter charts, a navies table and a sea battle log. Founding preview says whether your land touches the open sea. Budget shows the navy.
- Old saves: version 18 games load; harbors that already handled more trade than a small harbor are enlarged to fit, so nobody's trade shrinks.
- Real references: Liberty ship 10,865 DWT, 11 knots, $2M then (~$46M today); WWII shipping per soldier 5 GRT to a port, 9 for an assault landing; pier loading 1,500 t a day (800 with lighters); Fletcher destroyer 2,050 t, 36.5 knots, crew 329; water freight several to ~35× cheaper than road.
- File 1.40 MB, 17,458 lines. Season time about the same as v18. Checked by an independent review (17 findings, all fixed: a landing crash with two defending nations, voyages that could get stuck, lake harbors from old saves, too many divisions on one beach, training divisions sailing, unlimited warship purchases, lost orders when a harbor changed hands, news and panel texts) plus headless simulations, war-at-sea scenarios, the old-save test, the 40 save tests and the full browser suite.

### Version 20: depth in small changes (Maz: "add more depth in small changes over everything", with recommendations first)
Eleven small ideas were offered and Maz said "Go". Seven are in this version; the other four come next (see Next steps).
- **Work and unemployment.** Each town's workers (about half the people; soldiers under arms don't count) go to the farms first, then to the mines, plants and offices built there, then to the jobs in shops, transport, offices and public services the town can pay for: a share of its workers, more where roads reach a big market, 1.5 more for every job in industry, plus the public jobs that the healthcare and education laws pay for (more in the capital). The rest have no job and produce nothing. In a town cut off from the national market, people without a job farm family plots: they feed themselves but earn about 40% of a farm worker. Above 4% without work, stability falls (up to −12; welfare laws soften it). Economy panel: a Work section (workers by kind; jobless and newcomers by town); each town shows its work as a coloured bar; the Realm towns table has a "No job" column.
- **People move for work.** Each season about 15% of the jobless in a town (and some on family plots) move with their families to towns with work on the same roads, or from a cut-off town to the capital's market; families also leave towns where the food has run out. Exactly as many arrive as leave. The playback shows the move on the map ("+192k newcomers") and the dispatch says who moved where.
- **Jobless march** (new decision), when 9% of the nation, or 16% of a town on the national market, have no job: public works (costs money; public jobs for 3% of workers for 6 seasons), unemployment relief (−2% tax revenue; unemployment angers people half as much for 8 seasons) or disperse the march (−5 stability and a bitter memory).
- **Harvests and the national food reserve.** Every autumn the harvest comes in, good or bad (most years within ±6% of normal; a 7% chance of a bad year, −15%); a drought in summer means a poor harvest at best. The harvest sets what the farms can grow for a year: a nation with spare farmland hardly notices, one that only just feeds itself goes short. New law "Food reserve": none, 2, 4 or 8 months of food. In good years the state buys grain from its own farms ($1B per million people fed for a year, at most 1/16 of the target a season); about 1% a season is lost to damp and rats. When a harvest falls short, the towns joined to the capital (or the largest part still held if the capital falls) are fed from the store. New nations start with the reserve full. Rivals: agrarian powers keep 8 months, militarists 4, the others 2; they store more when war threatens and stop buying while in debt. Decisions: a record harvest (now only after a really rich harvest) can fill the granaries (+2 months); a drought can open them (−1.5 months, +1 stability). Economy panel: Food reserve section with the law; the Food number in the top bar says "+ store" while the reserve feeds people.
- **Smelters at the mines.** A metal mine can get a smelter ($3.5B and up, 2 seasons): the ore becomes metal on the spot (worth more; hauling cost halved) but it burns coal and electricity and needs workers. The site panel and the confirm card show the exact effect on output first; early on, with little coal, a smelter usually costs output, and the game says why (no coal to burn, or it would take coal and electricity from the power stations: open a coal mine first). Rivals build one only when it pays. Chimney mark on the map.
- **Every nation can fuel its first power station**: a nation that starts without a coal mine or oil field gets one opened near a town (fixes power stations standing idle from the first day, a v19 problem).
- **Veterans.** Divisions have experience from 0 to 1 in five levels: Green, Trained, Seasoned, Veteran, Elite; a division fights at ×0.9 when green up to ×1.4 when elite. The founding army starts trained (0.2), new divisions leave training at 0.15 and drill slowly in peace up to 0.25; each battle won adds 0.06, each lost 0.03; replacements arrive green and dilute it. Shown on the division card (with battles and wins), as a column in the division list, as gold chevrons over veteran counters on the map, and in battle reports.
- **Seasons in war.** Spring thaw: marches off the roads much slower, tank attacks −20%. Summer: the best season to campaign. Autumn rains: off-road marches slower, tank attacks −10%. Winter: off-road marches much slower (slowest in hills and mountains), attacks −15% (−30% in hills and mountains), troops without supply lose men twice as fast, frostbite in the mountains, small rivers freeze over, voyages 25% slower and landings 20% weaker. The generals plan with it; the season shows a weather icon; the Army panel and the dispatch say what it does; the march preview warns when the season slows a march.
- **"Why" cards.** Hover any number in the top bar (treasury, output, population, food, stability, army, prestige) or the season to see what makes it: budget lines; output by sector; births, deaths, war dead and who moved; harvest, fishing and the reserve; the biggest causes of stability; the army by type with experience and the season's effects; where prestige came from.
- Statistics: unemployment and food-reserve charts and comparisons.
- Old saves: version 19 games load (reserves start half full, reserve laws by character, the existing armies count as trained). World generation unchanged (checked on 6 seeds).
- Balance: the start is the same as v19; output grows a little slower in the first years (about 5% lower after 5 years: soldiers no longer count as workers, cut-off towns have fewer jobs, people move to where the work is) and after 20 years is within about 5% on average (single worlds differ more because of wars). National unemployment is usually under 1%; jobless towns appear where a town grows faster than its work.

### Version 37: trade between nations (Maz: "add more depth on managing trade with other countries"; after my reading of the old trade and my recommendations he chose packages 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6: "don't limit yourself because of the speed of the game … do everything")
- **Before**: every nation traded only with "the world" through ports and trade partners; no nation ever sold to another.
- **The merchants' book.** Once a season, after the harvest, every nation's merchants sign the next season's contracts (`engine/25b`): who sells what to whom and how much. A nation sells only what it has spare and buys only what it lacks. Merchants buy from the cheapest seller they can reach, by the **price at their border**: the world price, transport (road and rail across a border, caravans across up to 400 km of wild land, ships between two harbors on the open ocean, priced by the real sea distance), the buyer's import tax (none between trade partners) and, from beyond the map, the middlemen's 25% cut. They keep last season's seller unless another is 10% cheaper; a **preferred seller** chosen by the government counts 10% cheaper; buyers pay up to 10% more rather than buy from a nation they hate. Routes carry only so much: a border without a road 1–4 million tons a year, a trade road 10, caravans 1. Whatever no nation can sell or buy goes to the world market, within the old limits of harbors and trade partners. Valuable goods are loaded first. ### Version 39: places that live (Maz: "every village to have their own population and design", the rest of the "Places that live" ideas from the settlements research, and at least 3000 lines of honest, best work)
- **Research first** (`settlements-research.md`): how settlements began, why a place is chosen (wet point, dry point, defensive sites, the lowest bridging point, gap towns, harbors, crossroads), their shapes (nucleated, dispersed, linear), growth limited by food and by travel time, the "urban graveyard", central places and primate cities, migration, slums, boomtowns and ghost towns, shrinking cities, and how stubbornly cities survive. 23 ideas in three parts; Maz chose part 1.
- **Every village is a place of its own** (engine 10w): its own site, people (a share of its land's people: 2% on an ordinary site, more on a river, a bridge, a crossroads or good farmland, less on dry land, poor soil or without a road), look, and story (a line of its people every season and a log of what happened). It moves toward the size its site and fortunes allow a little each season: the town nearby doing well or badly, the young leaving farm villages as eras pass, villages near big cities filling with commuters, hunger, occupation, battles. Hamlets between the villages are worked out from the land each time (their own people and look, never saved), so every house cluster on the map has people. Villages' people are part of their land's people, so they change no economic number by themselves.
- **Ten looks**, from Maz's pictures and the research: clustered village (packed roofs, a green and a church), village among trees (red roofs in small groups under a canopy, ponds, footpaths), street village (houses along the stream or road with groups branching into the fields), village of walled yards (mud walls, round huts, sandy footpaths), hilltop village, fishing village (houses along the shore, boats on the beach, a quay), mining camp (barracks rows, spoil heaps, a siding), market village (two roads crossing at a square with stalls and inns), scattered farms (farmsteads in their fields), modern village (curving streets, closes, a roundabout, a terrace, flats and shops; from the Electronic Age near cities of 5M+). Ruins are drawn as roofless walls overgrown with trees. Chosen by climate (dry, temperate, wet), site, livelihood and era; village names follow the climate too.
- **The map painter** draws each village from two textures on the 6 km grid (position and size; look, direction, people and a random number): street villages follow their river or road, fishing villages turn their backs to the land, camps sit beside their pit, market villages sit on the road. - **Market villages** grow by themselves where roads meet, where a road crosses a river and at the road stop a day from the nearest town; a government can also grant a market charter.
- **Boomtowns, busts and ruins**: a new mine at a village makes a boom camp (fortune seekers; a share of the mine's workers and families live there; fever can break out); when the mine is worked out, a bust: the camp shrinks to a farm village or is abandoned. Land left without a nation leaves ruins; settlers come back to ruins later (or the government sends them). The town of a new mine is a boomtown for two years (crowded, mood −3); when its mine closes, its people hold on: they leave half as fast for three years (`cityStay`).

Rivals decide by their character; boom camps sometimes grow into towns this way.

Every town's site now changes its growth (up to +0.32% a year, down to −0.16%) and how hard it is to take (up to +15% for high ground, a river bend, a pass, dry ground in wet land, a bridge).
